1Toy ECC encryption and decryption

The scheme of Part 1 computes Q = kG on y² = x³ + 7. Here it is turned into a full encryption scheme of the ElGamal / ECIES kind (a Diffie–Hellman key agreement on the curve, followed by a keystream). It is a toy because the private key is restricted to a small range [1, 2bits], so that the attacks of Section 5 are feasible. You can choose the real secp256k1 parameters or the tiny curve over F17 used in Part 1.

The algorithm

  1. Setup. Public parameters: the curve, the generator G and its order n. The receiver picks a random private key d in the restricted range and publishes Q = dG.
  2. Encryption (needs only Q). The sender picks a fresh random r (ephemeral key), computes R = rG and the shared secret S = rQ. The keystream is KS = SHA-256(S.x ‖ 0) ‖ SHA-256(S.x ‖ 1) ‖ … and the ciphertext is (R, c) with c = m ⊕ KS.
  3. Decryption (needs d). The receiver computes S' = dR = d·rG = r·dG = rQ = S, hence the same keystream, and recovers m = c ⊕ KS.
KeyWho knows itRole
d (private key)receiver onlyNeeded to decrypt: turns R into the shared secret S = dR. Its secrecy is the whole security of the scheme.
Q = dG (public key)everybodyLets anyone encrypt for the receiver. Computing Q from d is easy, d from Q is the discrete-logarithm problem.
r (ephemeral key)sender, discardedMakes every encryption different and must never be reused. R = rG travels with the ciphertext.

The fields on the left are editable: change a hex digit and decrypt again, and you will see exactly the bit you changed in the output. This is the malleability weakness discussed in Section 6.

2Caesar cipher

Each letter is moved s places forward in the 26-letter alphabet: c = (m + s) mod 26. Decryption moves it back: m = (c − s) mod 26. Case is preserved; digits, spaces and punctuation are left untouched.

Only 25 keys change the text (s = 0 is the identity), about 4.7 bits of key space. The key is symmetric: the same s encrypts and decrypts.

4Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

Idea. Caesar only relabels letters, so the histogram of the ciphertext is the histogram of the language rotated by the key. We learn a model of the language from a corpus, try the 26 possible keys, and keep the one whose decryption looks most like the language. The scoring rules below differ in how much of the language they use.

  • Most frequent letter. Assume the most frequent ciphertext letter is the most frequent letter of the language (E in English): the key is the distance between them.
  • Chi-squared. For each key compare the 26 observed counts with the counts expected from the corpus, χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² / Ei, and take the key with the smallest value.
  • Bigram / trigram log-likelihood. Score a candidate plaintext by Σ log P(letter | previous one or two letters), with probabilities estimated from the corpus (add-k smoothing).
  • Exhaustive search + dictionary. Decrypt with all 26 keys and count occurrences of very common words (THE, AND, CHE, NON…).

Attack a ciphertext

Effectiveness against ciphertext length

For every length, many random fragments of the held-out passage are encrypted with random keys and attacked. A tie between several keys counts as a random guess among the tied keys. The random seed is fixed, so results are reproducible.

Effectiveness and limits

  • Length matters most. With a handful of letters the histogram is too noisy (and different keys can give equally plausible words), so every method fails often. With a few tens of letters the chi-squared and bigram scores are already almost always right, while the crude “most frequent letter” rule still needs several hundred letters.
  • Richer models need less text. Bigram scoring uses the letter-to-letter structure and works with much shorter ciphertexts than the plain frequency counts. Trigrams carry even more information but need more training data: with a small corpus most trigrams are never seen and the estimate becomes noisy (see the corpus-size experiment).
  • The model must match the text. The language and the style of the corpus matter. A corpus in the wrong language, or a ciphertext such as a list of numbers, a text with an unusual alphabet or one that avoids common letters on purpose, breaks the assumption.
  • It is ciphertext-only. Nothing about the plaintext is needed, but the attack works because Caesar preserves the statistics of the text. It would be useless against a cipher whose output looks uniform.

5Attacking the toy ECC through its restricted key space

The page creates a victim key pair for the message above. An eavesdropper knows the curve, G, the public key Q and the ciphertext (R, c). Because the private key lies in [1, N] with a small N = 2bits, the discrete logarithm can be found by search. Two attacks are implemented:

  • Exhaustive search. Compute G, 2G, 3G, … by repeated addition and stop when jG = Q. At most N steps, about N/2 on average, almost no memory. (The inner loop uses Jacobian coordinates, so no modular inversion is needed per step.)
  • Baby-step giant-step (Shanks). Let m = ⌈√N⌉. Store the “baby steps” jG for j = 1..m in a table, then walk the “giant steps” Q − i·mG until one lands in the table: then d = i·m + j. About 2√N operations and √N memory.

Once d is known, the message is decrypted exactly like the legitimate receiver does. The same code can also attack R instead of Q: the ephemeral key r comes from the same small range, and from r the shared secret is simply S = rQ, without ever touching d.

How the cost grows with the key size

Random keys of increasing size on secp256k1, 3 trials each. The number of group operations is what matters: exhaustive search doubles with every extra bit, baby-step giant-step only grows by a factor √2.

6Discussion

6.1 Statistical attack on Caesar versus search attack on the toy ECC

AspectCaesar: statistical attackToy ECC: key-space attack
What is exploitedRedundancy of the plaintext language, which Caesar leaves visible in the ciphertext.The tiny number of possible private keys, and the fact that Q = dG gives an exact test for a guess.
Input neededThe ciphertext, plus a model of the language.The public key (or R). The message and its language are irrelevant.
Dependence on message lengthStrong: short ciphertexts are ambiguous.None: even an empty message is attackable, because the public key alone suffices.
VerificationHeuristic: “does it look like English?”. Can fail or be tied.Exact: either jG = Q or not. Always correct.
CostAbout 26 × L operations, negligible.O(N) for exhaustive search, O(√N) for baby-step giant-step: depends on the key size.
Role of statisticsEssential.None. The ciphertext statistics are flat (Section 3), so there is nothing to learn from them.

Note that Caesar can also be broken by exhaustive search over its 26 keys. The statistics are needed only to automate the question “which of the 26 decryptions is the real one?”, because the attacker has no exact test. In the ECC case the public key provides that test, so no statistics are needed and the only question is how many guesses are required.

6.2 Why the same approach fails against a properly configured ECC scheme

  • Size of the search space. On secp256k1 the private key is uniform in [1, n−1] with n ≈ 2256. Exhaustive search needs about 2255 steps on average. The table in Section 5 shows that this is out of reach by many orders of magnitude, and so is the memory of baby-step giant-step (2128 table entries).
  • The best generic attacks only reach about 2128. Pollard’s rho needs roughly √(πn/4) ≈ 2128 group operations with negligible memory. For a generic group of order n, Ω(√n) is a proven lower bound (Shoup), and for well-chosen curves no faster classical algorithm is known: unlike integer factorisation, there is no sub-exponential index calculus.
  • No statistical shortcut. The keystream is a hash of a curve point, so the ciphertext is indistinguishable from random bytes. The frequency analysis of Section 4 has nothing to latch onto.
  • Caveats. “Properly configured” also means a key drawn uniformly from the full range with a cryptographic random generator, validated public points, and an authenticated encryption layer. Security is computational, not unconditional: it relies on the hardness of ECDLP, which a large quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm would break.

6.3 Other attacks, and where the weakness comes from

WeaknessCaused byExplanation
Exhaustive search, baby-step giant-stepRestricted key spaceImplemented above.
Pollard’s kangaroo (lambda) methodRestricted key spaceIt solves the discrete log when the exponent is known to lie in an interval of width w with about √w steps and almost no memory. It is the natural attack against a private key confined to a range, and is more memory-friendly than baby-step giant-step.
Attacking r instead of dRestricted key space (also for the ephemeral key)If r is drawn from the same small range, R = rG gives S = rQ directly, so the sender’s randomness is as weak as the receiver’s key. You can try it with the selector in Section 5.
Tiny subgroup on the F17 curveCurve choiceOn the Part 1 curve G has order 9, so there are at most 9 shared secrets and 9 keystreams: the ciphertext can be broken without the public key by trying each one.
MalleabilityDesign (no authentication)The ciphertext is a plain XOR, so flipping a ciphertext bit flips exactly the same plaintext bit, with no way for the receiver to notice (try the button in Section 1). A real scheme needs a MAC or an AEAD mode.
Reuse of rDesign / implementationIf the same r is used twice the keystream repeats and c1 ⊕ c2 = m1 ⊕ m2: the two-time pad, which falls to the same kind of statistics used on Caesar. This implementation draws a fresh r each time with crypto.getRandomValues.
Invalid-curve and small-subgroup attacksDesign (no validation of R)The point-addition formulas never use the constant 7, so a point of a different curve y² = x³ + b′ with small order is processed without complaint. An attacker who can see decryption results could learn d modulo those small orders. R should be checked to lie on the curve and in the right subgroup.
Weak key derivation contextDesignThe hash input contains only S.x and a counter: it does not include R, Q or a protocol label, and there is no integrity tag. Standard schemes such as ECIES bind these in.
Non-uniform keysImplementationA private key chosen from a predictable generator or a biased range loses security even if the range looks large. Here the range is public by construction, which is exactly what the toy attack uses.
In short. The key restriction causes the search attacks (exhaustive search, baby-step giant-step, kangaroo, attacking r). The lack of authentication, of point validation and of context in the key derivation come from the design of the toy scheme itself and would remain even with a 256-bit key, while the tiny subgroup is specific to the F17 curve.

6.4 Conclusions

  • Caesar and the toy ECC scheme are both reversible keyed transformations, but they belong to different worlds: a shared 26-value shift versus a public/private key pair with a randomised ciphertext.
  • Caesar fails because of statistics: the ciphertext preserves the structure of the language, so about a hundred letters are enough to recover the key.
  • The toy ECC fails only because of its key size: the ciphertext looks random, but a small key range can be searched in about 2bits/2 steps.
  • With a full-size key the search becomes infeasible, and what remains to get right is the implementation: randomness, validation, authentication.
